🏛️ The Real Mont Saint-Michel: A Field Guide

According to ninth-century church chronicles, Mont Saint-Michel exists because the Archangel Michael appeared to Bishop Aubert of Avranches in 708 CE, commanded him to build an oratory on a tidal granite rock, and, when the bishop dragged his feet, burned a hole directly into Aubert's skull with a heavenly finger. Convinced by this management style, Aubert started building, and by the thirteenth century Benedictine monks had stacked a three-story Gothic abbey known as La Merveille eighty meters above the bay. During the Hundred Years' War, English armies besieged the island for thirty years without success, and after 1793 the French Revolution repurposed the whole monastery as a high-security prison for unruly priests and political dissidents.

Getting to the rock today involves parking your car two and a half kilometers inland on the Normandy mainland and either walking across a sleek modern elevated bridge or cramming onto a free shuttle bus known as Le Passeur, which features hinges at both ends so the driver never has to turn around. Once through the medieval gate, ninety percent of humanity squeezes into the Grande Rue, a cobblestone lane barely wide enough for a wheelbarrow, where La Mère Poulard charges thirty-eight euros for a puffy wood-fired omelette that consists primarily of whipped air. Above the gift shops, climbing the 350 worn granite steps to the abbey cloister provides an unforgettable calf workout.

None of this monastic grandeur satisfied @DillDisaster, who posted a scorching one-star review titled 'A Briny Blot on Normandy's Landscape!' after arriving at the mouth of the Couesnon River and declaring the entire island a 'giant fermented cucumber.' It takes a remarkably specialized set of expectations to travel to Lower Normandy, cross a tidal bay where the ocean rushes in at the speed of a galloping horse, and complain that the eleventh-century Romanesque nave smells too much like vinegar. Yes, the lower stone ramparts get coated in slick green sea lettuce at high tide, and yes, the bay smells faintly of low-water mudflats, but no one in thirteen centuries has ever tried putting it on a pastrami sandwich.

💡 Practical Visitor Tip: Check the official tide tables before you go; if the tidal coefficient exceeds 110, arrive two hours before high tide to watch the sea completely sever the mount from France.
