🏛️ The Real Eiffel Tower: A Field Guide

Although Gustave Eiffel gets his name on the souvenir snow globes, the actual structural concept was dreamed up in 1884 by two of his senior engineers, Maurice Koechlin and Emile Nouguier, who sketched a thousand-foot open-lattice pylon in their spare time. Eiffel initially was not interested until architect Stephen Sauvestre dressed up the engineering sketch with decorative base arches and a glass pavilion on the first deck. Assembling the 18,038 wrought-iron parts required two and a half million rivets; workers heated each rivet in portable coal forges on the scaffolding and hammered them into place before they cooled and shrank tight. Every seven years, sixty tons of paint in three shades of brown are brushed on by hand to keep the iron from rusting into the Seine.

If you refuse to wait in the elevator queues and opt for the cheaper stair ticket to the second floor, you are in for 674 metal steps of existential reflection. Walking up inside the south pillar feels like ascending the fire escape of an industrial oil refinery while looking straight down through steel mesh at ant-sized pedestrians below. Halfway up the first leg, everyone's calves start cramping in unison, yet nobody wants to admit defeat in front of French schoolchildren jogging past them. Once you reach the viewing platforms, the romantic skyline of Paris is framed by heavy protective wire caging and dense clusters of couples taking forty-seven identical selfies while blocking the stairwell exits.

None of this structural metallurgy satisfied WiltingViolet, who traveled to the Champ de Mars in search of the "Eiffel Flower." In a review titled "Seriously disappointing bloom!", this frustrated botanist calls the tower "just a big metal weed" and complains that it has the "worst perfume ever" because it "smells like rusty nuts and bolts, not a garden." Granted, sixty tons of drying linseed paint and ten thousand tons of puddled iron do not give off the delicate bouquet of a Provencal lavender field. Still, shoving your nose against a riveted nineteenth-century girder and deducting stars for a lack of petals is a bold horticultural stance.

💡 Practical Visitor Tip: Buying the stair ticket up to the second floor saves you an hour in the ground-level lift queue and costs a fraction of the price, provided your knees are on speaking terms with you.
